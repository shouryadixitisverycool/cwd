What the end-sem asks, and what it skips

I went through every end-sem from 2022 onwards (2023, 2024 and 2025 papers with keys, plus the 2022 key) and the two surprise quizzes, and gave each question a topic. The 2023–25 papers come from the same setter: same layout, same cheat sheet, and questions repeated word for word. Together they are 300 marks.

2022 is left out of the counts: only its key survives and it shows no marks. Its 10 questions were 5 on inference (two of them hypothesis tests), 2 on MGFs and sums, 2 on functions/min-max and 1 on iterated expectation. That key looks like a different offering, since several questions are copied from ProbabilityCourse.com.

Asked every year: the core of the paper

  • MLE, 2–3 questions every year (20–30 marks). Gaussian mean and SD (2023), Gaussian variance with known mean (2024), Uniform[0,a] (2023) and Uniform[a,b] (2024 and 2025, word for word; the maximum is on the boundary, so setting the derivative to zero doesn't work), and Poisson rate (2023, then 2024 and 2025 word for word, with its MSE).
  • Bias / MSE of an estimator, every year. Bias of σ̂ or σ̂², MSE of the Poisson MLE, "define MSE and explain the bias–variance trade-off" (2025), and the MSE of three estimators of the mean (2025 surprise quiz, 10 marks).
  • Conjugate-prior Bayesian question, every year (10–15 marks). Beta–Bernoulli (2023), Gamma–Poisson (2024), Gamma–Exponential (2025): find the posterior and then the MAP. The keys give 7 marks for the posterior and 3 for the MAP, and they evaluate the normaliser as a Gamma function.
  • An MGF derivation, every year (10 marks). Gaussian MGF and sums of Gaussians (2023), shifted exponential (2024), Geometric and Exponential (2025), and the random-sum identity M_Z(t) = M_N(log M_X(t)) (2024). The keys always state the domain of t, and marks depend on it.
  • A convergence proof from first principles, every year (8–15 marks). Convergence in probability and in distribution (Laplace(n) in 2023, Uniform(5 ± 1/n) in 2025), mean square (Poisson(nλ)/n in 2023), and in probability plus almost surely via Borel–Cantelli (Xₙ = n w.p. 1/n², 2024).

Asked often

  • Simulation (2 of 3 years, 10 marks). Prove discrete accept–reject is correct (2023); Monte Carlo estimate of π with justification (2025). A "simulate a Markov coin from these uniforms" question (2024, and the 2025 surprise quiz) is really inverse transform.
  • Random vectors / Jacobian (2 of 3 years, 10 marks). The Jacobian for (X₁+X₂, X₁/(X₁+X₂)) (2023); X = AZ + μ: mean, covariance and PDF (2024). They also made up 22.5 of the 30 marks in the 2024 surprise quiz.
  • Sums by convolution. Erlang(2) by convolution (2025) and sums of Gaussians via MGF (2023).

Asked rarely or never

  • Topics 1–3 (counting, axioms, named distributions, single continuous RVs): never in an end-sem. They are Quiz 1 and midsem material, but the later questions use them all the time.
  • Functions of one RV, min/max: once (2024: log-normal PDF, max/min PDFs; 13 marks).
  • Iterated expectation: once (2025: a mixture, 10 marks), though the midsems ask it every year.
  • Numerical Chebyshev / Markov / CLT approximations: never in 2023–25. The CLT only shows up as "state it".
  • Covariance of scalar RVs, law of total variance: never.
  • Linear regression / least squares: never asked yet; it is new in this year's lectures. Hypothesis tests and confidence intervals: only in 2022.

What to expect this year

  • About 15–18 marks are free this year: Markov chains used to take them and are out of the syllabus. The likeliest places for those marks are inference, random vectors (which the 2026 Quiz 2 tested) and possibly the new linear regression lectures.
  • Order of preparation: inference (MLE including boundary cases, bias/MSE, posterior + MAP) → MGFs with domains → convergence proofs → simulation proofs and Gaussian vectors / Jacobians → regression → the rest.
  • He repeats questions word for word (2024 → 2025: the Uniform[a,b] MLE and the Poisson MLE + MSE). Every past-paper question is marked ★ Must-do in the topic guides above. The exceptions are the two 2022 hypothesis-test questions, which this year's lectures don't teach; they sit in an optional section.
  • Write answers the way the keys do: state the support or range first, name the method, justify each step (independence, monotonicity, LOTUS), give the MGF's domain, and box the final result.
